The tool list

Everything here is personal experience and personal preference. Nothing is sponsored: no affiliate links, nobody pays to be on this list, and I'm not interested in sponsorships. This is also not an exhaustive list of available tools — I've listed tools that are well made, last a lifetime, and are reasonably available. There are many more options out there; if something isn't here, it's for one of four reasons: I'm unfamiliar with it, it has limited availability, its price makes it inaccessible, or it's cheap garbage.

Buying links are in my order of preference — first link is the one I'd buy. Prices are what each seller listed in August 2026 and will drift. The core badges mark what I'd tell a new chairmaker to buy first. Spot something wrong or missing? Tell me.

Spokeshave corefrom $165

Controlled shaping and finishing tool: a small drawknife with a sole. A spokeshave is to a drawknife what a handplane is to a chisel.

  1. Lee Valley — Veritas flat spokeshave (and round-bottom) $165 (flat or round)Flat-bottom is the one every student needs; round-bottom is necessary for some concave surfaces, but if you have to pick one, get the flat-bottom. I find the Veritas friendlier to adjust than the Lie-Nielsen — and it has “speed holes” at the corners of the mouth for shavings to escape, a sore spot on most spokeshaves. Flat and round are $165 each; both were out of stock at last check (notify-me works, and the round shows a September restock).
  2. Lie-Nielsen — Boggs spokeshaves $165 flat / $195 curvedBronze-bodied, Brian Boggs pattern. The most common model among modern chairmakers.
  3. eBay — vintage Stanley No. 151 typically $30–$65The pattern the new ones are based on. Replacement blades are available from a few makers, like Hock.
Scorp / inshave corefrom $139

A drawknife that has been bent to hollow out surfaces. The typical seat-saddling tool.

  1. Lonon Tools — chair maker’s inshave from $355All of Jason’s tools are great.
  2. Lucian Avery — inshave (via Sawyer Made) about $300 incl. US shippingFlatter profile, small-batch production. A nice tool for a shallow saddle; not as hefty as the others. Sold exclusively through Sawyer Made in batches every three months — join the waitlist; about $300 including US shipping.

  4. Barr Tools — Mike Dunbar-style scorp $198Good steel, but the current runs have some geometry issues. Expect to regrind.
Travisher corefrom $126

A scorp with a sole. Can be used as a refining or finish tool for seats, or even for rough seat carving, depending on the design.

  1. The Windsor Workshop (James Mursell, UK) — wood or Delrin travisher £198–£234 inc VATWhat I would buy for a classroom. Ships to the US; the Delrin-soled Deluxe is forgiving enough that some people skip the scorp entirely.

Card scraper corefrom $11

A flexed steel card that follows the curve of a saddled seat or an arm and shears off the last tool marks and tearout — the cheapest edge tool in the kit.

  1. Lost Art Press — Crucible card scraper $25Curved so you don’t have to bow it with your thumbs — copied from the scraper Welsh chairmaker Chris Williams brought to his first US class.
  2. Highland Woodworking — Bahco 474 scraper $11–$12.50The original Sandvik steel. The thin .023″ flexes more; the .032″ is the stiff general-purpose card.
  3. Blue Spruce Toolworks — card scrapers from $24.99A few shapes and thicknesses. Tool steel instead of spring steel — offers very thick scrapers.

Boring & reaming

Cylindrical tenon cutter corefrom $115

Cuts a straight round tenon on the end of a leg, stick or stretcher.

  1. Lee Valley — Veritas power tenon cutters $115–$125 (5/8–1″)5/8, 3/4 and 1″ are the most common. I reach for 7/8 and 1-1/4 here and there. The little 3/8–1/2 minis are a nice luxury item. Sizes drift in and out of stock; the set of three is $329 when it’s in.
  2. The budget route — “Chairmaking on the Cheap(er): Leg Tenons” (Lost Art Press) freeChris Schwarz’s rundown of tenon-cutter substitutes — layout, plane, saw and rasp methods that cost next to nothing.
Tapered reamer (6°)from $85

Turns a straight bored hole into a tapered socket, so a matching tapered tenon wedges tighter the harder the chair is sat in.

  1. Tim Manney — 6° tapered reamer $305The one I’d buy. Batches roughly monthly; his newsletter gets a day’s head start on each drop.
  2. Elia Bizzarri — 6° chairmaking reamer $170 finished / $85 rawRiven hickory body, set-screw depth adjustment. Also sold in a set with three tenon cutters and a dowel plate ($560–$600).

6° tools (Manney, Bizzarri) go together; wide-angle tools (Veritas, 12.8°) go together. Mixing systems is how tenons end up loose. Tapered joinery is core if Windsors are your road — a stick chair can live on straight cylindrical tenons.

  1. Elia Bizzarri — 6° tapered tenon cutters from $140Leg (11/16), arm-stump and spindle sizes; custom angles to order. Pairs with the 6° reamers above.

Auger bits (WoodOwl) corefrom $14.5

WoodOwl’s chair-boring augers. Two patterns for one job — pick by what’s driving them.

  1. Lee Valley — WoodOwl tri-cut bits (for a brace) $26–$55.50 per bitLee Valley just titles these “Wood Owl Bits.” The lead screw self-feeds — perfect in a brace, wrong for a drill press. 7/16″ hex shank, which runs fine in many two-jaw braces; Lee Valley’s own three-jaw is the guaranteed fit. Sets: 3 bits $79, 6 bits $149.
  2. Lee Valley — WoodOwl OverDrive bits (for a power drill) $14.50–$28.50 per bitLee Valley’s own copy opens “Favored by chairmakers.” A short lead point instead of a lead screw, so they don’t self-feed — which is exactly why they behave in a cordless drill.
Bit bracefrom $20

The hand-powered drill chairmakers still prefer for big augers — slow, controllable, and it never strips a mortise.

  1. eBay — vintage Stanley, Yankee or Millers Falls $20–$50 typicalThe old Stanley and Yankee braces are great; I like the Millers Falls if you can find them. 8″ and 10″ sweeps are everywhere — I prefer a 10″, and bigger is better if you run 1″+ bits. A usable Stanley or Millers Falls asks $20–$50 these days; a Yankee 2101 runs $75–$120. Check the photos for a working ratchet and undamaged chuck jaws.
  2. Lee Valley — three-jaw chuck brace $106.50 (three-jaw) / $98.50 (two-jaw)About the only new braces on the market — French-made, composite handles, 10″ sweep. Lee Valley specs the three-jaw for hex shanks and the two-jaw ($98.50) for square tapers and small rounds — though plenty of two-jaw braces grip hex just fine in practice. Also available from McMaster-Carr.
Bit extension

Puts 18 inches between chuck and bit so you can sight a long drilling angle — and bore through a seat into an arm post.

  1. Make your own — Pete Galbert’s parts list (free) freeFree instructions with the exact McMaster-Carr part numbers — and he now sells a ready-made extension on the same page if you’d rather not build. The 1/2″ coupler route limits you to 1/2″-shank bits.
Dowel platefrom $57.15

Sizes riven pegs and small tenons exactly — drive an oversize blank through the plate and it comes out round.

  1. DFM Tool Works — dowel plate $57.15Has all the sizes — the English plate runs 17 holes, 1/8″ to 5/8″ in 1/32″ steps, hardened A2. Sold out at the maker at last check; the link is Amazon, where it’s in stock.

Laser levelfrom $30

A modern alternative to a T-bevel for drilling angles. Best results using two at once — a tripod for one, the other sits on the bench.

  1. Amazon — green-beam cross-line lasers $30–$120Green beams read better in shop light. Decent self-leveling units run $30–$70 and name brands $90–$160 — nothing about chair work needs the $200+ multi-plane units. Double-check the listing is actually green-beam; the search mixes in red ones.

Green woodworking

Froe core — green woodfrom $103.33

Splits a billet into chair blanks along the grain. A simple tool, but the geometry really matters — many modern ones are made by folks who don’t use them, and I find the angle too obtuse.

  1. Sharky (SharkyFST on Etsy) — hand-forged froe $137.78 (on sale $103.33)I generally think Sharky tools are overpriced crap, but their froe angle is lovely. The eyelet is a bit small and I find myself replacing the handles regularly — to me, that’s worth it for a froe that works. 52100 steel, 10″ edge, ships from Ukraine.
  2. Lonon Tools — froe $200Jason’s froe is listed, but the first batch is earmarked for the Handworks show — expect them to reach the store after that. I haven’t used one yet, but I bet it’s great.

Bench & assembly

Jack plane (No. 5) core — kiln-driedfrom $385

Flattens seat blanks, trues arms, tapers legs — the general-purpose plane of the kit. Mine travels with a smoother beside it.

  1. Lie-Nielsen — No. 5 jack plane $385The gold standard. I prefer the steel; the bronze is a premium option for people who want a heavier plane. Lie-Nielsen stocks in batches — sold out at last check, notify-me works.
  2. Lee Valley — Veritas Custom No. 5 $389I prefer Lie-Nielsen; some others prefer Lee Valley. LV tends to lean into innovation — LN has a way of perfecting the old ways.
  3. eBay — vintage Stanley No. 5 Budget option. Plan to spend some time getting it up to snuff. Replacement blades are widely available (Hock, Zen-Wu, and more).
Block plane core — kiln-driedfrom $200

Small handplane for anything from rough-shaping sticks to chamfering edges and finish work. Different models excel at different tasks.

  1. Lie-Nielsen — 60-1/2 adjustable-mouth block plane $200Compact size and high quality. The adjustable mouth makes this my favorite all-purpose block plane.
  2. Zen-Wu Toolworks — block plane $579An expensive specialty tool. The open, non-adjustable mouth and light weight make it my first choice for stick chair work — I’d take the LN if I could only have one, but if you already own a fine-mouth block plane, this thing is awesome.
Push sawfrom $105

Cuts on the push stroke. Beginners tend to find them more challenging than pull saws. There are many styles.

  1. Lie-Nielsen — dovetail saw $175Filed for rip cutting, but crosscuts OK in a pinch. If I could only have one push saw, I’d pick this one.
  2. Lie-Nielsen — carcass saw $175Slightly larger, filed for crosscutting. If I could have TWO push saws…
  3. Lee Valley — Veritas dovetail saw $105Less expensive; works quite well.
Pull saw corefrom $47.5

Pull saws cut easily, but they tend to deflect in the cut if not used properly. Very affordable, and replacement blades are inexpensive and easy to install.

  1. Lee Valley — traditional ryoba (rip + crosscut) $47.50Great value.

One good saw is core — and if I had exactly ONE saw, it’d be a ryoba: rip and crosscut in the same tool.
